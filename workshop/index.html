01 The workshop

I don't stop at the render. I cut the part, wire the driver, run the job, and find out what the drawing got wrong — then fix the drawing.

On the drawingEvery part starts fully defined in SolidWorks — sketches that can't drift, GD&T that says what actually matters, sheet-metal flat patterns the shop can run without calling. At ZMicro that meant enclosures dimensioned to MIL-STD shock, vibration and EMI; at home it means a 6-DOF arm whose every link and joint housing is my own geometry, not a downloaded kit.

On the metal5052-H32 forgives a tight bend; 6061-T6 cracks at it. Interlocking sheet-metal seams beat a screw down the centreline that would rattle, breach the EMI boundary and collide with the PCB. Heat-set inserts in printed bosses, PEM hardware in thin gauge, captive screws that survive a vibration table. The metal always gets the last word, so I ask it early with printed coupons.

On the windMotion is where mechanics meet firmware. My mini CNC taught me steps-per-mm, acceleration and feed rates until the machine stopped stalling; the robot arm uses position-reporting serial-bus servos so a hand-moved leader arm drives it live, and every session becomes training data for a policy that generalises instead of replaying.

03 From the bench

General arrangement, 6-DOF arm — drawing GA-001, revision B Side elevation · balloons · title block

The arm is being modelled link by link — base and flange, cylindrical joint housings with pocketed servos, capsule links with insert bosses — and every dimension on this sheet is one I actually had to decide: 140 mm upper arm, 110 mm forearm, a 4.2 mm bore for a 4 mm pivot boss. The drawing is redrawn each time the bench disagrees.
